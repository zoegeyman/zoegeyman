The Croissant Index

How easy is it to get a good croissant near you? I argue the answer quietly tells people where a place sits on the global ladder of "development."

The idea

A measure that isn't a metric

While living in Delhi for fieldwork, I kept a mental map of where to find a good croissant (ideally with a cappuccino). I picked my apartment partly for its distance to one. Over time I noticed that croissants, sushi and handloom clothing kept showing up in the same neighborhoods, worn and eaten by the same people.

The Croissant Index names that pattern. It is deliberately vague, because development is a comparison: who seems developed next to whom. Nobody consults the index. People feel it, in a split second, when they size up a café, a neighborhood or themselves.

I grew up in the D.C. suburbs feeling securely "developed." Then I went to France, ordered a pastry in rusty French, and got answered in perfect English. That flash of feeling behind is the index at work.

Suddenly frappuccinos are not just worse, but behind.

From the chapter

How it works

Three properties

01

It's relational

There's no fixed score. A place or person reads as developed only in comparison to someone else, and the comparison depends on who's looking.

02

It's instant

The judgment happens before any thinking does. A room, a menu or an accent places you and everyone around you in a hierarchy of taste.

03

It keeps moving

Once a signal spreads beyond the elite, it loses its value and a new one takes its place. McDonald's once signaled global modernity. Now it's craft and "authenticity."

From the field

Three moments

  1. Two people, one Tim Hortons

    Leaving a Delhi mall with colleagues, one of them lit up at the new Tim Hortons, where people had lined up for hours on opening day. To me it was a Canadian donut drive-through. Same store, two completely different readings, and each one placed us as a different kind of person.

  2. "Qwa-so!"

    For International Croissant Day, a Delhi mall posted a reel built on a viral TikTok joke about over-pronouncing "croissant." Laughing along teaches viewers two things at once: how to say it correctly, and how to look down on people who try too hard.

  3. Dinner at Taco Bell

    A friend teaching English in Delhi was invited out to an exciting new restaurant, hoping for a meal she couldn't get at home. Her colleagues took her to Taco Bell. Each side was reaching for the other's idea of the good life.

Beyond croissants

There's an index for everything

Once you see one, you start finding others. Each measures fluency in a different center of taste and power.

Croissant Index

Command of European taste: the café, the bakery, the right pronunciation.

Handloom Index

Indian craft recast as cosmopolitan. Wealthy Delhiites in naturally dyed kurtas, setting the terms instead of catching up.

Sushi Index

The small embarrassment of fumbling with chopsticks, and what we fear it says about us.

K-Index

South Korea left "developing" status in 1996. Today K-pop and K-beauty set global benchmarks.

Why it matters

What this means for understanding consumers

A brand means what its audience thinks it means. Tim Hortons is ordinary in Toronto and aspirational in Delhi. The same product can carry opposite status in two markets.

Status signals have a shelf life. As something becomes widely available, the people who adopted it early move on. Knowing where a product sits in that cycle changes how you position it.

People buy who they want to be. A croissant can stand for home, an indulgence, or proof that you belong. Close listening is how you find out which.

The stakes

The chapter ends with the collapse of USAID funding and the jobs it supported. The croissants are still there. Access to them, for many, is not. What happens when you've cultivated the capacity to aspire, then lose it?
